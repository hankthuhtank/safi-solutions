Dotted words open a plain-English definition.

Part IWhat it is

A mortgage is a loan where the house itself is the collateral: if the payments stop, the lender can take the home. You repay it in equal monthly payments, usually over 30 or 15 years.

The monthly bill is more than the loan, though. Lenders and budgets both work from PITI: principal and interest on the loan, plus property taxes and homeowners insurance, which your servicer usually collects into an escrow account and pays for you. Put down less than 20% on a conventional loan and PMI is added on top. HOA dues, if any, are billed separately.

The choices that set the price

Term. A 15-year loan has a higher payment but a lower rate and half the years of interest. On the example loan at today's 15-year average of 6.73%, the payment is $3,267 instead of $2,559, and total interest falls from $551,701 to $218,393.

Fixed or adjustable. A fixed rate never changes. An ARM starts lower for a set period, then moves with an index, within caps printed on page 3 of the Loan Estimate.

Loan type. Conventional loans up to the conforming limit ($832,750 for one home in most of the country in 2026) are the default. FHA loans accept lower credit scores and 3.5% down, but charge FHA mortgage insurance: 1.75% up front plus 0.55% a year for most borrowers, for the life of the loan if you put down less than 10%.

Your best tool is a form

Every lender must give you the same three-page Loan Estimate within three business days of applying, so offers line up side by side: rate and payment on page 1, closing costs and discount points on page 2 (section A), APR and TIP on page 3. Freddie Mac found that one extra quote saved borrowers about $1,500 over the life of a loan on average, and five quotes about $3,000.

Part IIHow it works

Every payment is the same. What it buys changes.

Each month the lender charges interest on whatever you still owe. The rest of your fixed payment goes to principal. Because the balance is largest at the start, the first payments are mostly interest: in the example, principal does not overtake interest until payment 249, in year 21.

This is called amortization. It is also why an extra dollar of principal early on is worth so much: it is never charged interest again.

  1. Monthly rate = annual rate ÷ 12. 7.40% ÷ 12 = 0.6167%.
  2. Interest this month = balance × monthly rate. $369,630 × 0.6167% = $2,279.39.
  3. Principal this month = payment − interest. $2,559.24 − $2,279.39 = $279.85.
  4. New balance = old balance − principal. Then repeat, 360 times.

PMI has three off-ramps

Under the Homeowners Protection Act, you can ask your servicer to cancel PMI once your balance reaches 80% of the home's original value (if you are current and have a good payment history). It must end automatically when the scheduled balance hits 78%, and no later than the midpoint of the loan. In the example that means you can ask in year 9 and it ends on its own in year 10. FHA mortgage insurance follows different rules.

Points buy a lower rate with cash

Discount points are prepaid interest: each one costs 1% of the loan and lowers the rate by an amount that varies by lender (a quarter point of rate is a common rule of thumb, not a standard). They pay off only if you keep the loan past the break-even point. A lender credit is the same trade in reverse.

Refinancing resets the clock

A refinance replaces your loan with a new one. A lower rate cuts the payment, but closing costs come due again, and a fresh 30-year term can mean paying interest for longer than you have left today.

Part VCommon mistakes

  1. Error

    Budgeting on principal and interest alone

    In the example, taxes, insurance and PMI add $717 a month to the $2,559 loan payment, 28% more. Repairs come on top.

    The fixBudget from the full payment on line 10, plus roughly 1% of the home's value a year for upkeep.

  2. Error

    Shopping the rate instead of the Loan Estimate

    A lower rate can hide discount points and fees in section A of page 2. The APR on page 3 folds those in.

    The fixRequest at least three Loan Estimates on the same day and compare page 2, section A, and the APR on page 3.

  3. Error

    Waiting for PMI to fall off on its own

    Servicers must stop PMI at 78%, but they don't have to remind you at 80%. In the example, waiting costs $2,310.

    The fixNote the month you will reach 80% and ask in writing. A big rise in your home's value may let you ask sooner with a new appraisal, depending on the loan investor.

  4. Error

    Buying points you will not keep long enough

    Points pay off slowly: often five years or more. Sell or refinance before then and the cash is gone.

    The fixRun the Points calculator with an honest guess of how long you will keep this loan, not how long you hope to keep the house.

  5. Error

    Counting only the monthly savings on a refinance

    Restarting at 30 years can raise your total interest even at a lower rate, and closing costs often get rolled into the balance.

    The fixCompare total remaining cost, and consider a shorter new term or keep paying your old payment amount.

  6. Error

    Extra payments that never reach principal

    Servicers can treat an unlabeled extra amount as an early payment of next month's bill, not a principal reduction.

    The fixMark extra money "principal only" and check that the next statement's balance dropped by that amount.

  7. Error

    Treating the pre-approval as the budget

    A pre-approval shows the most a lender will risk, and many programs allow total debt payments near half of your gross income.

    The fixSet your own ceiling from take-home pay and the rest of your goals, then shop below it.

  8. Error

    Ignoring the yearly escrow analysis

    If taxes or insurance rise, your payment rises too, even on a fixed rate. Home insurance premiums jumped about 12% in 2025.

    The fixRead the escrow letter, shop your homeowners policy every year, and appeal an assessment that looks too high.
