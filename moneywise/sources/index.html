Method and assumptions

Rounding

  • Loan payments are rounded to the cent each month, the way servicers post them; the last payment absorbs the leftover cents. Big figures on screen are rounded to the dollar, and the tape shows cents.
  • Tax under $100,000 of taxable income can differ by a few dollars from your return, because the IRS Tax Table taxes the middle of each $50 band. The tax calculator says when that applies.

Assumptions, form by form

  • Mortgage. PMI is charged on the original loan amount and ends when the scheduled balance reaches 78% of the original value (automatic under the Homeowners Protection Act), at 80% if you ask, and never later than the midpoint of the term. APR uses the actuarial method of Regulation Z, Appendix J.
  • Car loans. A new car is assumed to lose about 20% of its value in the first year and about 16% a year after that (roughly 40% left at five years). Sales tax is figured after the trade-in, as most states do.
  • Interest rates. APY = (1 + APR / n)n − 1 for n compounding periods a year. The rate-shock tool moves card, home-equity and savings rates one for one with the Fed; for savings that is the most you could expect, since banks usually pass on less, and later.
  • Credit. Interest accrues monthly at APR ÷ 12. Every debt gets its minimum first; the rest of the budget goes to the target debt, and a paid-off debt's payment rolls to the next. The minimum-payment example uses a common issuer formula: 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, at least $35.
  • Health insurance. You pay the deductible, then your coinsurance share, until the out-of-pocket maximum; premiums are paid on top and never count toward it. In-network care only.
  • Taxes. 2026 federal brackets and deductions; the child tax credit phases out by $50 per $1,000 of income over $200,000 ($400,000 joint). Paycheck withholding is approximated as the year's tax spread evenly; your employer's IRS Publication 15-T tables land close to this when your W-4 is current. State tax is a flat estimate you enter.
  • Savings and retirement. Returns are assumptions, not forecasts. Fees come out of each year's return: (1 + return) × (1 − fee) − 1, the SEC's method. Contributions arrive through the year, are capped at the IRS limit for your age, and the limits rise with inflation after 2026. "Today's dollars" divides by (1 + inflation)years.
