Dotted words open a plain-English definition.

Part IWhat it is

Health insurance is a deal with two prices. The premium is what you pay every month to belong. Everything else (the deductible, copays and coinsurance) is what you pay when you actually use care, up to a yearly ceiling called the out-of-pocket maximum.

Those four numbers trade off against each other. A low premium usually means a high deductible, and the reverse. Neither is "better": the right plan is the one with the lowest total for the year you are likely to have, and a worst case you could survive.

Premium
Paid every month, used or not. Never counts toward the deductible or the maximum.
Deductible
What you pay for covered care each plan year before the plan starts to share the cost.
Copay
A flat fee for a visit or prescription, often before the deductible is met.
Coinsurance
Your percentage of each bill after the deductible, until you hit the maximum.
Out-of-pocket max
The most you pay for in-network covered care in a plan year. Then the plan pays 100%. By law no more than $10,600 for one person in 2026 and $12,000 in 2027.
Open enrollment is coming

2027 plans can make one person pay up to $12,000 ($24,000 for a family) before the plan pays everything, up from $10,600. If you choose an HSA-eligible plan, you can put $4,500 (self-only) or $9,000 (family) into a HSA for 2027, pre-tax.

Part IIHow it works

Your share climbs a staircase, then stops.

  1. Every month: the premium, no matter what.
  2. First dollars of care: you pay 100% until the deductible is met. Copays and preventive care often skip this step.
  3. After the deductible: you pay your coinsurance share (say 20%) of each allowed amount; the plan pays the rest.
  4. At the maximum: the plan pays 100% of covered in-network care for the rest of the plan year. Then it all resets.

On an example plan with the average single deductible ($1,886, from KFF's 2025 survey of employers), 20% coinsurance and a $5,000 maximum, a $12,000 surgery costs you $3,909. A $45,000 hospital stay costs you $5,000: the maximum, not 20% of the bill.

Two things sit outside the staircase entirely: premiums, and care from out-of-network providers, which may cost far more and may not count toward your maximum at all.

Part VCommon mistakes

  1. Error

    Choosing on premium alone

    The cheapest monthly premium can cost the most in a year with a surgery, and the lowest deductible can cost the most in a healthy year.

    The fixCompare premium plus expected out-of-pocket for a light and a bad year, and make sure you could cover the worst case.

  2. Error

    Thinking the maximum includes premiums

    Your worst case is the out-of-pocket maximum plus a full year of premiums. On the example plan that is $6,500, not $5,000.

    The fixAdd twelve months of premiums to the maximum when you size your emergency fund.

  3. Error

    Going out of network

    Out-of-network bills can be far higher, may not count toward your maximum, and some plans pay nothing.

    The fixCheck the network for every doctor, lab and facility before scheduled care. For emergencies and some hospital care, the No Surprises Act limits you to in-network costs.

  4. Error

    Forgetting the reset date

    Deductibles and maximums start over at the beginning of each plan year, often January 1.

    The fixIf you have already hit your maximum, schedule elective care before the plan year ends.

  5. Error

    Assuming a checkup is always free

    Preventive care is covered with no cost in network, but tests or treatment for a new problem during the same visit can be billed as diagnostic care.

    The fixAsk before extra tests whether they will be billed as preventive or diagnostic.

  6. Error

    Leaving the HSA on the table

    An HSA is the only account that is pre-tax going in, tax-free while it grows and tax-free coming out for medical costs. The 2027 limit is $4,500 self-only.

    The fixContribute through payroll to skip Social Security and Medicare tax too, take any employer deposit, and invest what you do not need this year.

  7. Error

    Skipping the drug list

    A plan can look cheapest until your prescription sits in a high tier of its formulary or needs prior authorization.

    The fixSearch each plan's drug list for every regular prescription before you enroll.

  8. Error

    Paying a bill before the EOB arrives

    Billing errors are common, and the provider's bill can differ from what your plan says you owe.

    The fixWait for the EOB and pay what it says you owe; call the provider if the bill is higher.
