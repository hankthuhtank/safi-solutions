Part IWhat it is

Interest is the price of using someone else's money for a while. When you borrow you pay it; when you save you earn it. Nearly every decision on this site turns on one rate or another, so it pays to know which rate is which.

APR or APY? An APR is the yearly rate before compounding. An APY includes the effect of interest earning interest, so it is always at least as high. Banks advertise APY on savings (the bigger number) and APR on loans (the smaller number). At 5% compounded daily, the APY is 5.13%.

Fixed or variable? A fixed rate is locked for the life of the loan. A variable rate is an index plus a margin, so it moves when the index does. Most credit cards and home equity lines are variable.

Nominal or real? The stated rate is nominal. Subtract inflation and you get the real return: what your money actually gains in buying power. Today's average savings account pays 0.37% while prices rise 3.35% a year, a real return of about −2.9%.

Part IIHow it works

One rate at the Fed. Many rates in your wallet.

The Federal Reserve sets a target for one rate: what banks charge each other overnight. Anything priced off short-term rates follows within days. Long-term loans, like a 30-year mortgage, follow the bond market instead, which reacts to where investors think inflation and the Fed are headed over the next decade.

That split is why the headlines and your quotes can disagree. Between September 2024 and December 2025 the Fed cut its target 1.75 points, from 5.25–5.50% to 3.50–3.75%. Over the same months the 10-year Treasury rose from 3.72% to 4.14% (monthly averages), and the average 30-year mortgage went from 6.18% to 6.19%: no change at all. In September 2026 the Fed raised rates again and the 10-year climbed past 5%, taking mortgage rates to 7.40%.

Cards are tied to prime, but the margin on top is up to the issuer. The CFPB found the average margin over prime rose from 9.6 points in 2013 to 14.3 points in 2023, which is why card rates barely fell when the Fed cut.

Part VCommon mistakes

  1. Error

    Comparing an APR with an APY

    A loan quoted as an APR and a savings account quoted as an APY are measured differently. The APY is always the larger number for the same rate.

    The fixConvert both to APY (or both to APR) with the calculator before you compare.

  2. Error

    Expecting a Fed cut to lower your mortgage rate

    From September 2024 to December 2025 the Fed cut 1.75 points and the average 30-year rate went from 6.18% to 6.19%.

    The fixWatch the 10-year Treasury yield, not the Fed headlines, when you time a home purchase or refinance.

  3. Error

    Expecting a Fed cut to rescue a card balance

    Cards move with prime, but issuers set the margin on top, and margins have risen for a decade. A one-point cut saves $60 a year on a $6,000 balance.

    The fixPay the balance down, or move it to a 0% balance transfer only if you can clear it before the promotion ends.

  4. Error

    Leaving savings at the national average

    On $10,000 the gap between 0.37% and the 4.05% T-bill yield is $368 a year.

    The fixUse an FDIC-insured high-yield savings account, a money market fund or Treasury bills for cash you are not spending this month.

  5. Error

    Saving at 4% while carrying a card at 22%

    Every dollar of card debt costs about five times what the same dollar earns in savings.

    The fixKeep a starter emergency fund, then send the rest at the card until it is gone.

  6. Error

    Missing the catch in "no interest if paid in full"

    Deferred interest promotions charge every month of interest back to the purchase date if a single dollar is left at the end.

    The fixDivide the balance by the months in the promotion and pay at least that much every month.

  7. Error

    Forgetting that variable debt rises with rates

    A home equity line at prime plus 1 costs $400 more a year on $40,000 after a one-point increase.

    The fixPay down variable-rate debt first when rates are rising, or ask about fixing part of a HELOC balance.

  8. Error

    Ignoring inflation

    A return below inflation loses buying power. Today's average savings rate is about 2.9% a year behind prices.

    The fixJudge every rate by its real return: the rate minus inflation.
