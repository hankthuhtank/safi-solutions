Part IWhat it is

Your credit report is the record: every card and loan, its limit, its balance and whether you paid on time. Your credit score is a grade computed from that record. Lenders use the grade to decide whether to say yes and what to charge; landlords, insurers and some employers look too.

The most common score is the FICO score, from 300 to 850. The average was 714 in spring 2026. Your income, savings, job title and debit card use are not part of it; only what is on your credit reports counts.

Part IIHow it works

Two habits do most of the work.

Pay on time. Payment history is the biggest factor, and a payment is not reported late until it is 30 days past due. Set up autopay for at least the minimum so a busy month never becomes a late mark.

Keep reported balances low. Utilization is your balances divided by your limits, both on each card and overall. The catch most people miss is timing: issuers usually report the balance on your statement date, not after your payment. Spend $2,000 on a $5,000 card and pay it in full on the due date, and the bureaus may still see 40%.

  1. Day 1Cycle starts. Purchases add to the balance.
  2. Day 30Statement closes. This balance is usually what gets reported to the bureaus.
  3. 21+ days laterDue date. Pay the statement balance in full and new purchases cost no interest (the grace period).
  4. 30 days past dueOnly now can a late payment be reported, and it can stay seven years.

Paying off several debts: two orders

Pay every minimum, then send all extra money to one target. The avalanche targets the highest rate first and always costs the least. The snowball targets the smallest balance first, costs a bit more, and delivers quick wins. Researchers at Kellogg studied about 6,000 people in a debt-settlement program and found those who closed out small balances early were more likely to clear all their debt. The best order is the one you will finish.

Part VCommon mistakes

  1. Error

    Closing your oldest card

    It shortens your credit history and removes its limit, which raises utilization on the cards you keep.

    The fixKeep old no-fee cards open; put a small recurring bill on them so the issuer does not close them for inactivity.

  2. Error

    Carrying a balance to "build credit"

    Paying interest does nothing for your score. On-time payments and low reported balances do.

    The fixUse the card, pay the statement balance in full every month, and keep the reported balance low.

  3. Error

    Maxing out a card you pay in full

    The statement balance is usually what gets reported, so a card paid in full can still show high utilization.

    The fixPay most of the balance a few days before the statement closes.

  4. Error

    Paying only the minimum

    On $5,000 at 22.36%, a typical minimum takes 16 yrs 6 mos and $7,811 of interest. $192 a month clears it in three years.

    The fixPay a fixed amount you choose, not the shrinking minimum the issuer prints.

  5. Error

    Opening new accounts right before a big loan

    Each application is a hard inquiry and lowers the average age of your accounts, just when a mortgage or auto lender is pricing you.

    The fixHold off on new cards for six months to a year before a major loan. Rate-shopping one mortgage or car loan within a short window counts as a single inquiry.

  6. Error

    Never checking your reports

    A 2012 FTC study found one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their three reports.

    The fixPull all three free at AnnualCreditReport.com and dispute errors directly with the bureau.

  7. Error

    Co-signing a loan

    You are fully responsible for the debt, and every late payment the other person makes lands on your report too.

    The fixOnly co-sign for an amount you could pay off yourself without strain.

  8. Error

    Letting one bill slip past 30 days

    One reported late payment can cost a strong score many points and stays for seven years.

    The fixTurn on autopay for every minimum, then pay more by hand.
