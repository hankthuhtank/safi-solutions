The fine print, enlarged.

Ten things that cost people real money because nobody points them out. Figures use today's published rates.

  1. PMI ends on its own at 78%, but you can ask at 80%.

    On a median-priced home ($410,700) with 10% down at today's 7.40%, you can request removal in month 105, about 1 yr 3 mos before it would drop automatically. Servicers don't remind you. Mortgage →

  2. One point of rate is a lot of house.

    On that same $369,630 loan, 7.40% instead of 6.40% costs $247 more a month, $88,987 over thirty years. Mortgage →

  3. Your card follows the Fed. Your mortgage follows the bond market.

    Card APRs are priced off the prime rate, which moves the day the Fed moves. New mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield, which can rise even when the Fed cuts. Interest rates →

  4. The average savings account pays 0.37%.

    A 3-month Treasury bill pays 4.05%. On $10,000 that is $37 a year versus about $405. FDIC-insured high-yield savings accounts pay close to the T-bill rate. Interest rates →

  5. Your score sees your statement balance, not your payment.

    Most issuers report the balance on the statement closing date, so a card you pay in full can still show high credit utilization. Paying before the statement closes fixes it. Credit →

  6. The minimum payment is designed to last.

    Paying only a typical minimum (1% of the balance plus interest, at least $35) on $5,000 at 21.19% takes 16 yrs 3 mos and costs $7,363 in interest. Credit →

  7. The out-of-pocket maximum leaves out your premiums.

    For 2027 plans the legal ceiling is $12,000 for one person and $24,000 for a family, before a year of premiums. Out-of-network bills may not count toward it at all. Health insurance →

  8. A raise never lowers your take-home pay.

    Only the dollars above a bracket line are taxed at the higher rate. A $1,000 raise that crosses into 22% still leaves you with more than $700 of it after federal and payroll taxes. Taxes →

  9. Your 401(k) cuts income tax, not Social Security and Medicare.

    Traditional contributions come off your taxable wages, but the 7.65% FICA tax is still taken from every dollar you earn up to the wage base. Taxes →

  10. Fees compound too.

    The SEC's own example: $100,000 growing 4% a year for 20 years ends about $29,000 lower with a 1% fee than with a 0.25% fee. Check the expense ratio on every fund you hold. Savings & retirement →
