Part IWhat it is

Saving for retirement is mostly a contest between three forces: compounding, which grows money on its own; inflation, which shrinks what each dollar buys; and fees, which quietly take a cut every year. Tax-advantaged accounts and an employer match tilt the contest in your favor.

The best return you will ever get

Vanguard's 2026 study of its plans found the average employer match was 4.7% of pay and the average employee deferral 7.6%, yet some workers still contribute less than their plan's match requires. If your employer matches 50% of the first 6%, putting in 6% earns an instant 50% return on that money before any market gain.

Part IIHow it works

Time does most of the work.

Growth earns growth. Someone who invests $300 a month from 25 to 65 at 7% a year ends with about $787,444, having put in $144,000. Starting at 35 instead ends near $365,991: ten years later costs less than half the money. The Rule of 72 explains it: at 7% money doubles about every ten years, so the earliest dollars double the most times.

Two things work in reverse. Inflation means a dollar in 30 years buys far less than today (prices rose 3.35% over the last year). And every percent of yearly fees compounds against you, which is why the expense ratio of each fund matters.

How much can you spend later?

Morningstar's 2025 research puts the safe starting withdrawal rate at 3.9%: spend that share of your savings in year one, raise it with inflation each year, and the money has a 90% chance of lasting 30 years. Flexible spending, delaying Social Security and inflation-protected bonds can support more.

Part VCommon mistakes

  1. Error

    Not contributing enough to get the full match

    In the example, dropping from 6% to 3% gives up $192,231 of employer money and its growth by 67, on top of the saver's own smaller contributions.

    The fixFind your plan's match formula and contribute at least up to its top. Treat it as part of your salary.

  2. Error

    Cashing out when you change jobs

    An early withdrawal before 59 1/2 usually costs income tax plus a 10% penalty: on $20,000 in the 22% bracket, about $6,400 gone, plus every dollar it would have grown into.

    The fixDo a direct rollover to your new plan or an IRA.

  3. Error

    Ignoring fees

    Paying 1% instead of 0.50% a year costs the example saver $137,892 by 67.

    The fixCheck each fund's expense ratio; broad index funds often charge under 0.10%.

  4. Error

    Setting it once and forgetting it

    A contribution that never rises falls further behind your pay and the limits every year.

    The fixTurn on automatic yearly increases, or raise your percentage by one point with every raise.

  5. Error

    Skipping Roth while your tax rate is low

    Early in a career, many people are in the 10% or 12% bracket. Paying tax now at a low rate can beat paying later at a higher one.

    The fixSplit contributions between traditional and Roth if you are unsure; your plan may allow both.

  6. Error

    Selling after the market drops

    Locking in a loss turns a temporary decline into a permanent one, and missing the rebound is costly.

    The fixPick a mix you can hold through a bad year (a target-date fund does this for you) and keep contributing.

  7. Error

    Planning in future dollars

    $1,254,932 at 67 sounds like a lot; in today's dollars it is $503,312.

    The fixJudge every projection in today's dollars, like line 10 of the worked example.

  8. Error

    Parking long-term savings in cash

    Cash at the 0.37% national average loses buying power when prices rise 3.35% a year.

    The fixKeep an emergency fund in high-yield savings; invest money meant for decades.
