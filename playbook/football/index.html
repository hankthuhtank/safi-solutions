Q1 · The basics

Start here: the words

Read this first. These are the words the rest of the page assumes you know, each one defined before it is used to explain anything harder.

Core vocabulary25 words used everywhere below

Snap

The moment the center gives the ball to the quarterback and the play begins.

Line of scrimmage

The imaginary line running through the football before the snap. The offense and defense line up on opposite sides of it.

Down

One play. The offense normally gets four downs to gain 10 yards and earn a new first down.

Formation

Where the offensive or defensive players line up before the snap.

Personnel

Which types of players are on the field. Example: “11 personnel” means 1 running back, 1 tight end, and 3 wide receivers.

Alignment

The exact place a player lines up within a formation.

Motion

One offensive player moving to a new location before the snap. Teams use it to change leverage, create speed, or help identify man vs zone coverage.

Shift

Two or more offensive players move before the snap, then stop and become set in a new formation.

Route

The path a receiver is supposed to run on a pass play.

Concept

A group of routes or blocking rules designed to work together. A “play concept” is the idea behind the play, not just one player’s assignment.

Coverage

The defense’s plan for defending receivers and areas of the field against a pass.

Man coverage

Each coverage defender is mainly responsible for a specific offensive player.

Zone coverage

Defenders are mainly responsible for areas of the field and react to receivers entering those areas.

Blitz

The defense sends extra rushers after the quarterback instead of using them in normal coverage.

Pressure

Any pass rush that forces the quarterback to hurry, move, or change the throw. A pressure does not have to end in a sack.

Box

The area close to the line of scrimmage where most run defenders line up.

Leverage

A player’s positional advantage. For example, a defender with inside leverage is positioned to make inside routes harder.

Gap

A running lane between offensive blockers. A-gaps are closest to the center; B-gaps are farther outside.

Front

How the defensive linemen and nearby linebackers are arranged before the snap.

Safety

A defensive back who usually lines up deeper than cornerbacks. Do not confuse the position with the two-point scoring play also called a safety.

One-high / two-high

A quick way to describe the deep safety picture: one deep safety or two deep safeties.

Play action

The quarterback and running back fake a run before the quarterback tries to pass.

RPO

Run-pass option. The quarterback can hand the ball off or throw a quick pass based on what a specific defender does.

Read

The player or area the quarterback watches to decide where the ball should go.

Checkdown

A short, safer pass option used when deeper routes are covered.

Q1 · The basics

The game at a glance

Football is a territory-and-possession game. The offense tries to advance the ball and score; the defense tries to stop the advance, force a punt, create a turnover, or hold the offense to a field-goal attempt.

ScoringNFL values
ScorePointsHow it happens
Touchdown<span class="num">6</span>The ball is carried into, or caught in, the opponent’s end zone.
Extra-point kick<span class="num">1</span>After a touchdown. In the NFL the ball is snapped from the 15-yard line, making it a 33-yard kick.
Two-point try<span class="num">2</span>After a touchdown, run or pass it in from the 2-yard line instead of kicking.
Field goal<span class="num">3</span>Kicked through the uprights. Kick distance ≈ yards to the goal line + 17 (10-yard end zone + 7-yard hold).
Safety<span class="num">2</span>The offense is downed in its own end zone. The team that gave it up then free-kicks from its own 20.
Defensive two-point return<span class="num">2</span>The defense returns a blocked kick or turnover on a try all the way to the other end zone.
The flow of a gameTwelve ideas

Down

One play. A series starts at first down. The offense normally has four downs to gain 10 yards and earn a new first down.

Line of scrimmage

The imaginary line through the ball before the snap. Most alignment and offside rules are built around it.

First down

A new set of downs, usually earned by gaining the required yardage.

Drive

One possession: every play from the moment a team gains the ball until it scores, punts, turns it over, or the half ends.

Touchdown

Six points. The ball is possessed in or carried across the opponent’s goal line.

Field goal

Three points for a successful kick through the uprights.

Safety

Two points for the defense when the offense is responsible for the ball becoming dead in its own end zone under the rules.

Extra point / try

After a touchdown, the scoring team attempts a one-point kick or a two-point offensive play.

Turnover

A change of possession created by an interception, fumble recovery, or failed fourth down.

Punt

A kick used to surrender possession in exchange for field position, commonly on fourth down.

Red zone

The area from the opponent’s 20-yard line to the goal line. Space compresses, so route concepts and run fits change.

Two-minute situation

Late-half offense/defense where clock preservation becomes as important as yardage.

Game structureClock, timeouts, overtime
  • A football field is 100 yards between goal lines, with 10-yard end zones at each end.
  • The offense has 11 players and the defense has 11 players on the field.
  • The offense can run, pass, option, or kick depending on down, distance, field position, score, and time.
  • Football strategy is mostly about numbers, space, leverage, matchups, and forcing defenders to choose.
  • NFL games are four 15-minute quarters. Each team gets three timeouts per half, and the clock stops automatically at the two-minute warning of each half.
  • The offense must snap the ball before the 40-second play clock expires (25 seconds after certain stoppages).
  • Overtime (regular season, since 2025): one 10-minute period. Both teams get a possession unless the first one ends in a defensive score; if still tied, the game can end in a tie. Playoff overtime continues until there is a winner.

NFL rules are the reference point for this guide. College, high-school, youth, and video-game rules differ in some details.

Q2 · The offense

Offensive positions

Eleven players, three groups: the offensive line protects, the backfield handles the ball, and the receivers stretch the field. Tap any player to see the job.

JobsPosition by position

Quarterback (QB)

Receives the snap, handles the ball on most plays, makes pre-snap adjustments, reads coverage, throws passes, and may run.

Running back (RB)

Primary ball carrier on many run plays. Also blocks for the quarterback, catches short passes, runs receiving routes, and can move before the snap as the motion player.

Fullback (FB)

Lead blocker / short-yardage runner in offenses that use one; less common in spread systems.

Wide receiver (WR)

Primary outside/slot pass catcher. Runs routes, blocks on perimeter runs, stretches the defense with speed, and may move before the snap to change the defensive look.

Slot receiver

Receiver lined up inside another receiver instead of near the sideline. This alignment gives more room to break either inside or outside.

Tight end (TE)

Hybrid blocker/receiver. Can align inline, in the slot, out wide, or in the backfield.

Center (C)

Snaps the ball and usually helps set protection or blocking calls.

Guard (G)

Interior offensive lineman. Important on inside runs, pull schemes, and pass protection.

Tackle (T)

Edge offensive lineman. Often responsible for elite pass rushers and edge-setting defenders.

Eligible receiver

A player allowed to catch a forward pass under formation and numbering rules.

Ineligible receiver

Usually an offensive lineman; cannot normally be the first player to touch a forward pass beyond the line of scrimmage.

Q2 · The offense

Personnel and formations

Personnel tells you who is on the field; formation tells you where they line up. The two-digit code counts running backs first, then tight ends; whatever is left of the five eligible players are wide receivers.

Packages & shapesThe language on the sideline

10 personnel

1 RB, 0 TE, 4 WR. Very spread-out, pass-friendly personnel.

11 personnel

1 RB, 1 TE, 3 WR. The modern all-purpose package because it can run or pass without substituting.

12 personnel

1 RB, 2 TE, 2 WR. Creates heavier run looks while still threatening the pass.

13 personnel

1 RB, 3 TE, 1 WR. Heavy package for short yardage, goal line, or play-action.

20 personnel

2 RB, 0 TE, 3 WR. Useful for two-back formations, option football, spreading the backs out as receivers, and pre-snap motion.

21 personnel

2 RB, 1 TE, 2 WR. Traditional pro-style / I-formation family.

Shotgun

QB stands several yards behind the center. Helps passing vision and spread run concepts.

Pistol

QB is shallower than shotgun with RB directly behind. Preserves downhill run threats while keeping QB off the line.

Under center

QB takes the snap directly from the center. Helps hard play-action and traditional run timing.

Singleback

One running back with no fullback. Can be under center or in shotgun.

I-formation

QB under center with FB and RB stacked behind. Strong downhill run/play-action identity.

Trips

Three eligible receivers line up on the same side. This forces the defense to decide how it will cover an overloaded side of the formation.

Bunch

Three receivers line up close together. Their releases can cross and overlap, making it harder for defenders to follow receivers cleanly in man coverage.

Empty

No running back is lined up in the backfield next to or behind the quarterback. Five eligible receivers are spread across the formation, which can make pressure and coverage easier to identify.

Condensed / nasty splits

Receivers align closer to the formation. Helps crossing routes, crack blocks, and two-way releases.

Motion

A player moves before the snap. Used to identify coverage, change leverage, create speed, or alter the run count.

Shift

Multiple players move and reset before the snap. Used to change formation or force defensive communication.

Q2 · The offense

The run game

Every run is a blocking plan plus a path for the ball carrier. Zone runs block areas and let the back choose; gap runs (power, counter) pull linemen to build a wall at one hole.

Run familiesFifteen concepts

Inside zone

Linemen block areas and combinations rather than a fixed defender. RB presses an interior landmark and reads the flow for a cut.

Outside zone / stretch

The line moves laterally to stretch the defense. RB reads the edge, vertical cut, or backside cutback.

Duo

Downhill run that can resemble inside zone but uses more vertical double-team concepts; RB often reads a linebacker.

Power

Gap scheme featuring down blocks and a pulling guard leading through the intended hole.

Counter

Misdirection gap scheme. Initial action goes one way while pullers lead the ball the other way.

Trap

A defender is intentionally left unblocked at first, then hit by a pulling lineman.

Toss / sweep

Ball gets to the perimeter quickly. Success depends on edge leverage and blocking in space.

Pin-pull

Certain blockers pin defenders inside while uncovered linemen pull around the edge.

Draw

Looks like pass protection at first, then hands the ball to a runner. Useful against aggressive pass rush.

Read option

QB reads an unblocked defender and either gives to the RB or keeps the ball.

Speed option

QB attacks the edge with a pitch relationship to a back, reading the force defender.

QB power / counter

Designed run using the quarterback as the ball carrier to gain a numerical advantage.

Jet sweep

Fast horizontal motion receives the ball at speed to threaten the edge.

End-around

Receiver takes the ball after the initial backfield action and runs around the opposite edge.

RPO

Run-pass option. The line blocks a run while the QB reads a defender and can throw a quick attached route instead.

Q2 · The offense

Routes and pass concepts

A route is one receiver’s path. A concept combines routes so that one defender has to choose between two threats: whichever way he goes, the quarterback throws to the other.

Routes & conceptsTwenty-seven entries

Go / fade

Vertical route. Forces a defender to run deep and can create sideline one-on-ones.

Slant

Quick inside break. Excellent against cushion and some man leverage.

Out

Breaks toward the sideline. Useful for timing and boundary access.

In / dig

Breaks inside, usually at intermediate depth. Attacks windows between linebackers and safeties.

Curl / hitch

Receiver pushes vertical then stops or works back to the QB. Good against soft zone.

Post

Breaks toward the goal post. Attacks middle-field leverage and split safeties.

Corner

Breaks toward the back corner. Often stresses Cover 2 or high-low concepts.

Seam

Vertical route through the inside channel. Attacks between safeties or down the hash.

Drag / shallow

Low crossing route. Great for man coverage and creating yards after catch.

Wheel

Starts toward the flat then turns vertically. Creates matchup stress on linebackers or safeties.

Option route

Receiver changes the break based on defender leverage/coverage. Requires QB-WR chemistry.

Mesh

Two shallow crossers intersect, creating natural traffic versus man and easy zone sits.

Flood / sail

Three-level stretch to one side: flat, intermediate out/corner, and deep route.

Smash

Short hitch/out underneath with corner route over it. Designed to high-low a Cover-2 corner.

Stick

Short outside read combining a stick/option route, flat route, and vertical clear-out.

Spacing

Multiple short routes settle in separate zone windows, giving the QB simple completions.

Levels

Multiple in-breaking routes at different depths stress linebackers horizontally and vertically.

Drive

Shallow crosser paired with an intermediate dig. Creates a high-low on underneath defenders.

Dagger

Vertical clear route opens space for a deep dig behind linebackers.

Mills

Post plus deep dig. Can stress a single-high safety.

Four verticals

Four receivers attack deep seams and outside lanes, stretching deep coverage.

Y-Cross

Deep crossing route from TE/slot with complementary vertical/flat routes.

Snag

Triangle concept with a corner route, flat route, and snag/sit route.

Scissors

Two deep routes cross (a post and corner) to stress two-high safeties.

Screen

Throw behind or near the line to a runner/receiver with blockers releasing ahead.

Play action

Run fake before pass. Tries to move linebackers and safeties out of passing windows.

Bootleg

QB rolls away from run action after a fake, usually with routes at multiple levels.

Q2 · The offense

Pass protection

More blockers means more time, but fewer receivers. Protection is the trade-off between the two.

Five-man protection

Only offensive linemen protect. Creates five eligible receivers but fewer blockers.

Six-man protection

RB or TE stays in protection, reducing route count to handle pressure.

Seven-man / max protect

Two extra protectors stay in, typically for deep play-action or heavy pressure.

Half-slide

Part of the line zones/slides one way while the other side handles man rules.

Full slide

Line moves in one direction to account for a pressure front; backside issues may require RB help.

Hot route

Immediate route adjustment used when the offense cannot block every rusher.

Sight adjustment

Receiver/QB recognize pressure or leverage and convert the route accordingly.

Chip

RB/TE briefly hits an edge rusher before releasing into a route.

Pocket

Protected area around the quarterback. Interior depth and edge width determine escape lanes.

Sack

QB tackled behind the line while attempting to pass or before escaping as a runner.

Scramble

QB leaves the designed pocket after the pass play develops.

Beating pressureRules of thumb
  • Against heavy blitz, the ball often must come out faster.
  • Screens, quick game, slants, and hot routes punish defenders who vacate coverage space.
  • Deep shots require time; time usually requires protection help or winning one-on-ones up front.

Q3 · The defense

Defensive positions

Three levels: linemen at the ball, linebackers behind them, defensive backs (cornerbacks and safeties) deepest. Packages swap linebackers for defensive backs as offenses spread out.

JobsPosition by position

Defensive tackle (DT)

Interior defensive lineman who controls gaps, disrupts runs, and pushes the pocket.

Nose tackle (NT)

Interior defender aligned near the center, common in odd fronts. Often absorbs double teams and controls A-gaps.

Defensive end (DE)

Edge or interior-edge defender depending on scheme; sets the edge, rushes the passer, and plays run contain.

EDGE

Modern umbrella term for a primary outside pass rusher, whether formally a DE or outside linebacker.

Middle linebacker (MIKE)

Central linebacker; often involved in front checks, run fits, hook-zone coverage, and communication.

Weak-side linebacker (WILL)

Usually faster linebacker aligned away from the strength; pursues runs and covers space.

Strong-side linebacker (SAM)

Often aligns to the tight-end or formation strength; may set an edge, blitz, or cover.

Cornerback (CB)

Covers receivers, plays deep/flat zones, supports the run, and contests outside routes.

Free safety (FS)

Deep safety with range; often protects the middle of the field and helps erase deep mistakes.

Strong safety (SS)

Safety who may play closer to the box, cover tight ends, blitz, or rotate deep depending on scheme.

Nickel defender

Fifth defensive back, usually matched against slot receivers or used as a versatile coverage/blitz player.

Dime defender

Sixth defensive back, used in obvious passing situations or matchup packages.

Gaps are lettered from the center out (A, B, C, D). Techniques number where a defensive lineman lines up on a blocker. Together they describe a front in two words.

A-gap

Space between center and guard.

B-gap

Space between guard and tackle.

C-gap

Space outside the tackle, often between tackle and TE if present.

D-gap

Outside the tight end in heavy formations.

0-technique

Defender aligned head-up over the center.

1-technique

Defender shaded to one shoulder of the center.

2i

Inside shade of the guard.

3-technique

Outside shade of the guard; classic penetrating defensive tackle alignment.

4i

Inside shade of the tackle.

5-technique

Outside shoulder of offensive tackle.

Wide 9

Edge defender aligned very wide to attack pass-rush angle / set hard edge.

4-3 front

Four defensive linemen, three linebackers as the base structure.

3-4 front

Three defensive linemen, four linebackers, often with edge rushers standing.

4-2-5

Four linemen, two linebackers, five DBs. Popular versus modern spread offenses.

3-3-5

Three linemen, three linebackers, five DBs; flexible pressure and coverage structure.

Over front

Defensive line strength shifted toward offensive strength.

Under front

Front shifted away from strength, often creating a favorable weak-side edge alignment.

Bear front

Interior covered heavily, often with a defender over center and both guards to restrict inside runs.

Two-gap

Defender controls blocker and is responsible for either adjacent gap based on the run.

One-gap

Defender attacks a specific assigned gap.

Spill

Force a run wider so pursuit can finish it.

Box / force

Keep the runner from escaping outside; turn the ball back toward help.

Q3 · The defense

Coverage families

The number in “Cover 2 / Cover 3 / Cover 4” usually describes how many defenders are responsible for the deepest part of the field. Man coverage follows people; zone coverage guards areas.

Cover 0

Man coverage with no deep safety help. Enables pressure but is vulnerable to explosive plays if anyone loses.

Cover 1

Man underneath with one deep middle safety. Flexible and aggressive; crossers/picks/mismatches can stress it.

Cover 1 Robber

One safety plays deep while another drops into an intermediate middle zone to rob in-breaking routes.

Cover 2

Two deep safeties; cornerbacks and underneath defenders cover flats/hooks. Classic weaknesses can include the deep middle seam and sideline “hole” depending on technique.

Tampa 2

Cover 2 variation where the middle linebacker carries vertically through the deep middle, effectively helping close the traditional middle weakness.

Cover 3

Three deep thirds with four underneath defenders in common spot-drop versions. Strong balanced shell and common early-down call.

Cover 3 Match

Pattern-match version where zone defenders convert to man-like rules based on receiver releases.

Cover 4 / Quarters

Four deep quarters. Can defend verticals well while allowing safeties to trigger aggressively based on route/run rules.

Palms / 2-read

Two-high match coverage where defenders read the releases of No. 1 and No. 2 receivers and exchange responsibilities.

Cover 6

Quarter-quarter-half: quarters principles on one side, Cover 2 principles on the other.

Cover 2 invert

Safeties can rotate down and corners take deep halves, disguising the coverage shell.

Bracket / double

Two defenders combine to take away one dangerous receiver.

Zone blitz

Defense sends unexpected rushers while dropping traditional linemen and playing zone behind the pressure.

Q3 · The defense

Blitzes, pressure and run fits

Pressure is about numbers and surprise: send one more rusher than the protection can block, or rush four from places the offense did not expect.

Blitz

Sending one or more extra rushers beyond the normal front.

Fire zone

Zone pressure often using five rushers and three-deep / three-under coverage.

Double A-gap pressure

Two linebackers threaten the gaps on either side of the center, stressing protection communication.

Nickel blitz

Slot defender attacks from the edge/interior.

Safety blitz

Safety enters the pressure, often from a disguised depth.

Simulated pressure

Shows pressure but rushes only four, replacing expected rushers with unexpected ones.

Creeper

Four-man pressure using a nontraditional fourth rusher while another front player drops.

Stunt / twist

Two defensive linemen exchange rush lanes to create confusion and picks.

Contain

Rush with responsibility not to let the quarterback escape outside.

Spy

Defender mirrors a mobile quarterback rather than committing immediately to rush or zone.

Run fit

Assigned gap / leverage responsibility when the offense runs.

Cutback

Backside lane that opens when defenders over-pursue the initial flow.

Pursuit angle

The path a defender takes to intersect the runner rather than chase directly behind.

Q3 · The defense

Reading the defense

Use this in order. Do not try to identify the entire defense at once. Start with the easiest visual clues, then confirm them after the snap.

  1. Count the deep safeties. One deep safety is a one-high look; two deep safeties are a two-high look. This does not tell you the final coverage by itself, but it narrows the possibilities.
  2. Count the box. The box is the area close to the line of scrimmage where most run defenders line up. Compare those defenders with the offense’s available blockers.
  3. Look at the cornerbacks. Are they close to the receivers (press) or giving several yards of space (off coverage)? Are they lined up inside or outside the receiver?
  4. Use motion. Motion means moving one offensive player before the snap. If one defender follows that player across the formation, man coverage becomes more likely. If defenders pass the receiver from one area to another, zone coverage becomes more likely.
  5. Look for possible blitzers. A defender standing close to the line or showing an obvious rush posture may be threatening pressure. Ask whether the offense has enough blockers to account for them.
  6. After the snap, confirm the safety rotation. Defenses disguise their intentions, so the pre-snap picture can change immediately after the ball is snapped.
  7. Read the defenders your play is designed to stress. Good pass concepts usually force one or two specific defenders to choose between two threats.
  8. Take the easy answer. If the defense gives you a safe five-yard completion or an efficient run, you do not need to force a deep throw.

In real football, coverages are frequently disguised, matched, split-field, or adjusted by formation. The beginner labels are a starting point, not the complete language of defensive football.

Q4 · Game management

Situational football

The same play can be smart or reckless depending on down, distance, field position, score and clock.

1st and 10

Entire playbook is available. Offenses value efficiency and staying ahead of the chains.

2nd and short

Great shot-play down because a failed deep attempt still leaves manageable third down.

2nd and long

Defense can anticipate pass but must respect draw/screens.

3rd and short

Run fits, QB sneak, quick game, picks/rubs, and compact formations become important.

3rd and medium

Passing concepts with defined first-down landmarks matter.

3rd and long

Defense can play sticks coverage, pressure, or drop deep; offense often needs protection plus route depth.

4th down

No punt if offense chooses to go for it; must gain line-to-gain yardage or score.

Goal line

Field is compressed. Heavy personnel, pick concepts, fades, slants, and QB run threats matter.

Red zone

Less vertical space, tighter windows, more matchup and leverage football.

Two-minute offense

Sidelines, clock stops, hurry-up, spikes, timeouts, and pre-called concepts become central.

Four-minute offense

Team with lead tries to keep clock running and earn first downs.

Backed up

Offense near own goal line must avoid sacks, holding, turnovers, and safeties.

Prevent defense

Deep coverage designed to prevent explosive scores, often conceding underneath completions.

Hail Mary

Desperation deep pass into or near end zone at end of half/game.

Victory formation

QB kneels to run out the clock when opponent lacks enough timeouts.

Two-minute warning

An automatic stoppage when two minutes remain in each half. It works like a free timeout for both teams.

Kneel-down math

Each kneel uses a snap plus up to 40 seconds of play clock. With no opposing timeouts, three kneels can run out roughly two minutes.

Q4 · Game management

Special teams

Kicks trade possession for field position. The NFL rebuilt the kickoff in 2024 to bring returns back safely, and tweaked it again in 2025 and 2026.

Kickoff

Starts each half and follows scores. Since 2024 the NFL uses the “dynamic kickoff”: the kicker is at his own 35, the other ten coverage players line up at the receiving team’s 40, and no one moves until the ball hits the ground or a player in the landing zone.

Landing zone

The area between the receiving team’s goal line and 20-yard line. A kickoff must land here (or in the end zone) to be a legal, returnable kick.

SINCE 2025

Touchback (kickoff)

A kickoff that goes into the end zone and is downed there. Since 2025 the ball comes out to the 35-yard line; a kick that lands short of the landing zone is spotted at the 40.

Kick return

Return team tries to create a lane and maximize starting field position.

Punt

Kicker drops and punts ball to flip field position.

Punt return

Returner can catch and advance unless fair catch is signaled.

Fair catch

Returner signals not to advance in exchange for protection after the catch.

Field goal unit

Snapper, holder, kicker, and protection execute a timed kick.

Long snapper

Specialist who snaps punts and placekicks.

Gunner

Fast coverage player released outside on punts.

NEW 2026

Onside kick

A short kick the kicking team hopes to recover. It must be declared in advance and uses a special alignment. Since 2026 it can be declared at any point in the game (previously only in the fourth quarter).

Fake punt / fake field goal

Special-teams formation turns into a run/pass play.

False start

Offensive player makes an illegal movement simulating the start of the play.

Holding

Illegal restriction of an opponent. Offensive holding is especially common around blocks.

Pass interference

Illegal contact that significantly hinders an eligible receiver’s opportunity to catch a forward pass under the applicable rules.

Illegal contact

Certain prohibited defensive contact beyond the allowed area before a pass is thrown.

Roughing the passer

Illegal contact against the passer under protection rules.

Neutral-zone infraction

Defender enters neutral zone and causes reaction/contact under rule definitions.

Delay of game

Offense fails to snap before the play clock expires or other delay conduct occurs.

Intentional grounding

QB throws forward pass under pressure without satisfying requirements that make the throw legal.

Personal foul

Serious illegal contact, usually 15 yards.

Unsportsmanlike conduct

Non-football or prohibited conduct penalized by officials.

Decline

Team chooses not to accept an opponent’s penalty because the play result is better.

Offsetting fouls

Qualifying fouls by both teams can cancel for enforcement purposes.

2026 officiating note: league officials in New York may now advise on-field referees about disqualifications for flagrant football and non-football acts, and a flag can be added if one was not thrown.

OT · Game mode

Madden and football-game mode

Video games reward the same logic as the real sport. Learn the thinking once and it carries across every edition.

Play smarterTransferable habits
  • Build a tiny call sheet first: one inside run, one outside run, one man-beater, one zone-beater, one pressure answer, and one deep shot.
  • Learn one formation deeply before jumping through 20 formations. Know where your slant, flood, mesh, inside zone, and vertical concepts live.
  • Pre-snap: count safeties, inspect corner depth, count the box, then motion a receiver if you need another man/zone clue.
  • Against one-high man looks, bunches, crossers, drags, corner routes, and option routes can create separation.
  • Against Cover 3, attack curl/flat conflicts, seams, flood concepts, and underneath spacing.
  • Against Cover 2, stress the deep middle, seams, and the sideline hole between corner and safety when the game representation allows it.
  • Against heavy pressure, use quick throws, RB screens, TE delays, max protection, or move the pocket.
  • On defense, start by learning one user role (usually a linebacker or safety in the middle) and understand what route you are trying to remove.
  • Blitz less randomly. Pressure is strongest when it attacks a protection weakness or forces a fast throw into a coverage trap.
  • Use audibles as answers, not decoration: run vs light box, quick pass vs soft coverage, max protect vs pressure, shot play vs favorable matchup.
  • Clock management wins games: use chew clock with a lead, conserve timeouts when trailing, and know when the sideline matters.

Video-game mechanics and controls change by edition. This section teaches football logic that transfers across versions rather than promising version-specific exploits.

Rulebook watch

New for 2026

Rules move every spring. These changes were approved at the NFL Annual League Meeting and apply to the 2026 season, alongside the 2025 changes that are still new to most fans.

2026 season

Onside kicks at any time

A team may now declare an onside kick at any point in the game. Previously it was allowed only in the fourth quarter. It still has to be declared, with the special onside alignment.

2026 season

Kickoff setup-zone tweak

The receiving team gets more flexibility in how many players stand off the restraining line inside the 5-yard setup zone, with at least one player required near each sideline.

2026 season

Replay help on ejections

The league’s officiating department can consult with on-field referees on disqualifications for flagrant football and non-football acts, and a flag can be thrown if one was missed.

Since 2025

Touchbacks on kickoffs to the 35

The dynamic kickoff was made permanent and a kickoff into the end zone that becomes a touchback now puts the ball on the 35-yard line (up from the 30) to encourage returns.

Since 2025

Both teams get the ball in overtime

Regular-season overtime now matches the playoffs: both teams get a possession unless the first ends in a defensive score. The period stays 10 minutes, so games can still end tied.
